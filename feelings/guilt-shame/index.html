Guilt & Shame

Why you don’t see “guilt” or “shame” in the main feelings list

On this site, you won’t find guilt or shame in the core feelings list. That isn’t because those experiences aren’t real or important. It’s because allneeds.app is built on a particular way of looking at human experience:

Observation → Feeling → Need/Value → Strategy

Words like guilty and ashamed tend to bundle several of these layers together at once:

  1. What happened (the observation),
  2. What you feel in your body (emotion and sensation),
  3. What you tell yourself about who you are (“I’m bad,” “I don’t deserve to belong”).

Psychologists usually classify guilt and shame as self-conscious moral emotions—experiences that arise when we evaluate ourselves against internal or social standards of right and wrong.[1][2][4] ([PMC][1])

Common distinction

  • Guilt: focused on a specific behavior (“I did something wrong”).
  • Shame: focused on the self as a whole (“I am wrong / bad / unworthy”).[2][3][4] ([Taylor & Francis Online][2])

What research observes

  • Guilt is often linked to repair and making amends, whereas shame tends to promote withdrawal, hiding, or appeasement.[1][2][4] ([PMC][1])
  • Neuroimaging meta-analyses show both reliably recruit the anterior insula and networks tied to social pain and self-evaluation.[5] ([Preprints][3])

That makes guilt and shame important to take seriously. But for a tool that wants to keep observations, feelings, needs/values, and strategies distinct, it creates a design problem: guilt/shame words simultaneously name an emotion and carry a verdict about the self. That verdict can make it harder to see the underlying needs/values clearly.

So instead of treating guilt and shame as basic feeling entries, this page invites you to unpack them into observations, more basic feeling words, and the needs/values, wishes, and grief underneath.

You might still see guilt and shame as labels in body-cue research or search results (because the literature uses those words), but when you click through on this site, you land here so you can translate them into the building blocks allneeds.app uses.

Translations for guilt and shame

Body / immediate feelings

heavy chest, tight stomach, restlessness or nausea, agitation, sadness, fear, preoccupation, regret. Studies mapping bodily sensations report distinctive patterns for self-conscious emotions like guilt and shame, with strong activation in the torso, head, and face.[6][7] ([Academia][6])

Needs/values + wishing & grieving

integrity, honesty, fairness, care for others, contribution, learning, responsibility, reliability.[1][2][3] ([PMC][1])

Wishing you had found a strategy that honored those needs for everyone, grieving that you didn’t.

A translated version might sound like: “When I think about what I did, my chest feels heavy and my stomach knots. I feel scared and regretful, because I value honesty and care for this person, and I wish we had found a way to act that honored both their needs and mine. I’m grieving that we didn’t find that strategy this time.”

  • Is there a step of repair you’d like to take now?
  • What support or skills would you need in the future to see more possibilities?

Body / immediate feelings

hot face, wanting to hide, collapsed posture, looking down, feelings of smallness, terror, overwhelm, loneliness, or numbness. Bodily-mapping studies suggest shame and related states involve strong sensations in the head, chest, and sometimes gut, consistent with wanting to shrink or disappear.[6][7] ([Academia][6])

Needs/values + wishing & grieving

belonging, acceptance, dignity, being seen accurately, safety, inclusion, contribution.[1][2][4] ([PMC][1])

Wishing to be seen and still included, grieving moments where that didn’t happen.

A translated version might sound like: “When I think about that moment, my face burns and I want to disappear. I feel exposed, scared, and alone, because I deeply need belonging and dignity, and I wish there had been a way for me to be honest and still be held with care. I’m grieving that we didn’t find such a strategy.”

  • Whose standards are you being measured against?
  • What kind of community would honor your needs for dignity and belonging?
  • What new strategies or boundaries might protect those needs instead of attacking yourself?

The philosophy of the site: needs as values

Throughout allneeds.app, the word “need” is used in a specific sense. Here, needs is effectively a synonym for basic human values—the deep things human beings require for psychological growth and integrity, like safety, honesty, belonging, dignity, rest, play, understanding, meaning, contribution, and autonomy.

This aligns with research on basic psychological needs, which treats certain values as essential psychological nutriments: when they are supported, well-being and healthy motivation increase; when they are chronically frustrated, distress and defensive patterns increase.[10][11][12][13][14] ([Self-Determination Theory][4])

Feelings are the signals that arise as these needs/values are more or less supported, and strategies are the concrete things we try to care for those needs/values for ourselves and others.[10][14] ([Self-Determination Theory][4])

If you want examples of the kinds of needs/values this site works with, explore the Needs page and notice which words resonate.

A strong claim: it is possible to include everyone’s needs

The site makes a stronger assumption: for any situation, there exists at least one strategy that could care for the needs/values of everyone involved. This is a normative stance, inspired by integrative negotiation approaches that focus on interests (needs/values) and generating creative options before assuming conflict.[15][16] ([University of Hawaii][5])

Research on integrative or “win-win” negotiation shows that when people surface underlying interests and brainstorm options, mutually beneficial solutions are found more often than expected.[15][16] ([University of Hawaii][5])

Translating that spirit here: when needs appear to be in conflict, treat it as a sign of limited information, imagination, support, or time, not literal incompatibility. In principle, there may always be some way—perhaps unusual, staged over time, or requiring more community support—to respect everyone’s humanity and basic needs/values.

Guilt and shame often show up right at the edge of this: sensing something vital was not cared for, without yet seeing how it could have been.

Wishing & grieving

Wishing: “I wanted a strategy that held all our needs”

A big part of what people call guilt or shame is often a kind of wishing: “I wish we had found a way of acting that cared for everyone’s needs—mine and theirs.” That wish shows you care about your own needs and limits and the needs and dignity of others.[1][2] ([PMC][1])

Try asking: What do I wish had been possible here?

  • I wish I had spoken honestly and stayed kind.
  • I wish I had set a boundary and remained connected.
  • I wish I had rested before I snapped.

That wish points to specific needs/values (rest, respect, care, honesty, fairness, belonging, etc.) and to a belief—however faint—that a strategy could exist that doesn’t discard anyone. You’re allowed to want that, even if you’ve rarely seen it in practice.

Grieving: when we didn’t find that strategy (yet)

Sometimes we simply did not find the strategy that would have included everyone’s needs at that time—maybe we didn’t know how, were exhausted or scared, lacked information or safety, or only realized later what was at stake.

When we see this clearly, a natural response is grief, rather than a life sentence of self-condemnation. Grief says something we value didn’t happen and somebody’s needs were not cared for. In this logic, grief is not proof you are bad; it’s proof you care deeply about life, relationship, and integrity.

Chronic, global shame is linked with more distress and avoidance, whereas behavior-focused guilt is linked with constructive repair attempts.[1][3][4] ([PMC][1])

Grief can guide us toward repair (if possible and safe), learning about what we needed and didn’t have, and future strategies that are more resourced and imaginative.

Strategies and ideas

A note on language, choice, and imagination

If guilt and shame are the words that feel true for you, you are welcome to use them. This site isn’t here to police your vocabulary; it is here to offer a different lens:

  • What you’re calling guilt or shame is packed with feelings, needs/values, wishes, and grief.[1][2][3][4] ([PMC][1])
  • It is okay to wish there had been a strategy that honored everyone’s needs.
  • It is okay to grieve when you didn’t (or couldn’t) find it.
  • In principle, such strategies do exist, even when they’re far beyond what any of us can see alone.[15][16] ([University of Hawaii][5])

From there, the invitation is not to decide whose needs must be sacrificed, but to keep growing imagination together—so more and more often, we can actually find strategies that honor everyone’s needs, including yours.
