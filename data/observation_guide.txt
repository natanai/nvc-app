Guide to Crafting Purely Observational Statements in NVC
Observation vs. Evaluation: Marshall Rosenberg’s Perspective
In Marshall Rosenberg’s Nonviolent Communication (NVC) model, the first component is observation, which means describing what actually happened without any added judgment or interpretation[1]. Rosenberg emphasizes that we must “clearly observe what we are seeing, hearing, or touching… without mixing in any evaluation.” In other words, stick to the facts. For example, instead of saying “The principal has a big mouth,” which is an opinion, Rosenberg would ask: What did the principal say or do?[2]. An observation might be: “The principal spoke for 20 minutes about personal stories during the staff meeting.” This distinction is crucial: “He has a big mouth” conveys a value judgment, not a concrete action[2].
Rosenberg often quotes philosopher J. Krishnamurti: “Observing without evaluating is the highest form of human intelligence.” Yet Rosenberg admits this is difficult for most of us—our minds habitually leap to evaluations[3]. NVC doesn’t ask us to stop evaluating entirely; it asks us to separate observations from evaluations when we communicate[4]. You can still have opinions, but state the factual observation first and keep your opinion distinct. NVC discourages broad, static generalizations and prefers descriptions specific to time and context[5]. By doing so, we describe reality as a process (something that happened at a certain time and place) rather than labeling someone with a fixed trait[6].
Why Separating Observation from Evaluation Matters
When we blur observation with evaluation, people tend to hear it as criticism[7]. A statement like “You’re always late and inconsiderate” mixes a factual element (“late”) with an evaluation (“inconsiderate”). The listener will likely become defensive or hurt, and miss the core of your message. Rosenberg warns that combining observation and evaluation “decreases the likelihood that others will hear our intended message”[7]. In contrast, a pure observation (e.g. “You arrived 20 minutes after our agreed time”) is more neutral and clear. Observations stated this way are less provoking because they simply describe an event rather than judging it[8][9]. Research on communication confirms this effect: judgments and accusations trigger defensiveness, whereas neutral descriptions help keep dialogue open[10][8].
Sticking to observations is not just a polite nicety – it’s foundational for empathy and problem-solving. In the NVC process, you follow an observation with your feelings and needs. If your initial observation isn’t clear and agreed upon, the conversation can derail into arguments about what “really” happened (“That’s not true, I wasn’t inconsiderate!”)[11]. A clear, specific observation creates a shared starting point. Indeed, communication experts note that “observations are specific to time and context – a detective or camera could verify them as facts”, which makes it easier for others to hear you without feeling attacked[8]. By mastering this skill, you set the stage for the other person to listen to your feelings and needs rather than fight over your portrayal of events[11].
Academic insight: A 2023 literature review of NVC highlights that one need not eliminate all evaluations, but “it only requires that the observations be separate from the evaluations”[12]. This separation is a key reason NVC can improve relational outcomes. In practice, training in NVC (which starts with observing without evaluating) has been shown to increase empathy and communication effectiveness in participants[13]. By learning to state observations neutrally, individuals improved their ability to connect and reduced conflict escalation, validating Marshall Rosenberg’s approach in academic studies[13].
Key Principles of Pure Observation in NVC
Before we dive into the step-by-step process, keep these core principles in mind:
•	Describe what a video camera would capture: A camera or audio recorder can record what was said and done, not your interpretations. “A tape recorder or camera can record the facts of an event, but it can’t record an evaluation,” Rosenberg often noted[14]. If your statement contains guesses about someone’s motives or character, it’s not camera-friendly. For example, a camera can show “Charlie stepped in front of others in the queue,” but it cannot show “Charlie was selfish.” The first is observable; the second is an interpretation[15].
•	Be specific and pinpoint a time/context: Avoid vague generalizations like “always” or “never.” Instead, ground your observation in a particular moment. Rosenberg calls static generalizations (e.g. “You never do your share”) a form of evaluation that NVC tries to avoid[5]. If something happens frequently, state the frequency or timeframe: e.g. “three times this week” instead of “often”. This specificity makes your statement more factual and less accusatory[16]. “Always, never, ever, frequently, seldom…” – these words are red flags in observations[16]. Replace them with concrete metrics or examples (more on this in the steps below).
•	No name-calling or labels (even subtle ones): Words that categorize or judge a person (good, bad, careless, lazy, unprofessional, aggressive, etc.) indicate evaluation. Even positive labels like “My father is a good man” are subjective evaluations, not observations[17]. Focus on what the person did that leads you to that opinion. For instance, rather than “She was disrespectful,” an observation would be “She rolled her eyes while I was talking and walked out of the room.” By leaving out the label “disrespectful” and just describing the action, you stick to the observable facts.
•	Avoid implying intent, cause, or justification: In the observation step, do not add phrases like “for no reason,” “to get back at me,” “because you don’t care,” etc. Those dive into analysis of why the person acted and will automatically feel like criticism. For example, “John was angry with me for no reason” assumes he had no valid reason – that part is your evaluation. The pure observation could be “John told me he was angry yesterday”[18] or “John pounded his fist on the table yesterday”[9]. Save any discussion of motives or your interpretations for later (or better yet, focus on how it made you feel, not why they did it).
•	Keep observations free of your feelings and needs (initially): In NVC you will eventually express feelings and needs, but not in the observation sentence. An observation is purely external. If you say, “When you interrupted me, I felt disrespected,” the first clause is an observation (“you interrupted me [at that moment]”), but the second clause introduces your feeling. In the observation builder, stick to the first part only: what you saw/heard. (Feelings come in the next step of communication, and you can certainly express them—just keep them separate from the factual statement of what happened[1].)
With these principles in mind, let’s break down a foolproof, fill-in-the-blank process for creating a solid observational statement.
Step-by-Step: How to Formulate a Pure Observation
1.	Identify the specific event you want to describe:
Start by pinpointing when and where the behavior occurred, and who was involved. Narrow it to a single incident if possible. For example, ask yourself: “What exactly did I observe? When and where did it happen?” The goal is to set a clear context. Instead of “You don’t help out at home” (vague generalization), frame it as “Yesterday evening (when), in the kitchen (where), I saw the dishes from dinner left on the counter”. This anchors your statement in a concrete moment. Marshall Rosenberg stresses making observations “specific to time and context” rather than broad generalizations[5]. By zeroing in on one episode, you reduce ambiguity. (Imagine you’re selecting a particular scene in a movie of your life – you want the other person to know which scene you’re talking about.)
2.	Describe what a camera or microphone would capture:
Write down what you saw or heard, as if you were a reporter or a camera recording the situation. Stick strictly to observable actions, words, or facts. Use neutral verbs that describe actions (e.g. “said,” “did,” “left,” “took,” “slammed,” “smiled,” etc.) and, if relevant, quote the exact words used. The idea is that any third-party observer would agree with your description. For instance, “You raised your voice to above 80 decibels” might be too technical, but “You shouted at me, saying ‘This is all your fault!’” is an objective recounting (with a direct quote for clarity). By contrast, “You yelled at me disrespectfully” sneaks in an interpretation (“disrespectfully”). Test yourself: could a video camera capture this on film or a mic record it? A camera can see someone shaking their fist, but it cannot document “aggression” – that’s an interpretation[19]. It can record a person saying “I hate you”, but not that the person “was being mean.” This “camera lens” guideline is a classic NVC teaching[14]: if your words go beyond what a camera sees/hears, you’re likely evaluating. Try to include concrete details: who, what, when, where. For example: “At 3 PM today (when), I saw you (who) <u>shut the door of my office</u> without knocking and walked away (what happened).” This is a clear observation of behavior, with no extra commentary.
3.	Avoid trigger words that indicate evaluations:
Now review what you wrote. Remove any words that convey judgment, opinion, or subjective interpretation. Certain words are hallmarks of evaluation. These include:
4.	Adjectives about personality or character: e.g. rude, selfish, lazy, irresponsible, immature, arrogant, disrespectful, good/bad, etc. If you used any descriptors like that, replace them with descriptions of the actual behavior. For example, if you wrote “He was disrespectful,” ask “What did he do that I label disrespectful?” You might change it to “He interrupted me twice while I was speaking”. Similarly, “My colleague was unprofessional” could become “My colleague checked his personal phone twice during the meeting and missed a question directed at him.” The revised versions describe actions, not character[15].
5.	Adverbs like “too much,” “too loud,” “slowly,” or “unfortunately”: These often inject your opinion. “Janice works too much,” is an evaluation (what is too much?). Perhaps you mean “Janice worked 60 hours last week”. Quantify it instead of judging[20]. “He drove recklessly” could be “He drove at 90 mph on a 50 mph road.”
6.	Generalizations: “always, never, often, rarely, constantly,” etc. These words are rarely factual because they gloss over specifics. If you catch any of these, replace with a specific instance or a number. For example, “You always ignore my texts” might become “In the last week, you didn’t respond to 3 of my text messages”. NVC trainers note that words like “always, never, frequently, seldom” invite defensiveness – it’s better to cite the exact frequency or example[16]. Even the word "often" (as in “My son often doesn’t brush his teeth”) is considered an evaluation, because “often” is vague and subjective. In one NVC exercise, “My son often doesn’t brush his teeth” was rephrased as “Twice this week, my son didn’t brush his teeth before bed.”[21]. Notice how the revision paints a clearer picture.
7.	Phrases that guess motive or intent: e.g. “on purpose,” “to annoy me,” “for no reason,” “just to spite me.” These belong to our interpretation of why the person did it. They do not belong in an observation. If you wrote something like “She left the party without saying goodbye to hurt me,” drop the part in italics. The bare observation is “She left the party without saying goodbye.” Maybe you felt hurt and assume it was intentional, but the intention is not observable. Similarly, “He cancelled our plans because he doesn’t care” should be just “He cancelled our plans an hour before we were supposed to meet.” Stick to what happened; omit the “why.”
After editing, read the statement again and check for any remaining subjective words. Each word should represent something you could physically sense or a direct quote of someone’s words. If not, refine it. This step requires honesty with yourself – it’s easy to smuggle judgments in subtle ways. Take your time to purge those out. The result should sound plain, even somewhat boring or factual, like a news report. (That’s a good thing at this stage!)
1.	Use precise, concrete language (quantify when possible):
A purely observational statement often involves measurable or specific terms. Think of details like numbers, frequency, duration, volume, distance, etc., if they help paint the picture. This makes your statement more undeniable. For example, instead of “You’re playing your music really loud,” you could say “I can hear your music from my room with the door closed, and the bass makes the floor vibrate.” Instead of “She is often late,” say “She was more than 10 minutes late to four out of the last five team meetings.” Quantifying removes ambiguity. As one NVC guide advises, “stay away from words like always, never, frequently, seldom” and give specific examples or data[16]. If a behavior happened multiple times, state how many times, or give the last couple of instances. This level of specificity leaves little room for argument about the facts. It transforms subjective claims into concrete observations that the other party can verify or at least recognize. (They may not have counted the instances, but hearing “four of the last five meetings” is much clearer than “often.”) By quantifying or specifying, you effectively fill in the details that make an observation solid.
2.	Check for mutual observability (the neutrality test):
Now ask yourself: “Could another person present at that time agree this happened as described? Would the person I'm speaking to recognize it without immediately arguing?” This is a litmus test for your statement’s objectivity. If you predict that someone might respond “That’s just your opinion” or “No, that’s not what happened,” then there might still be evaluation or ambiguity in your description. Tweak it until it’s as undeniable as possible. For instance, if you say “You were texting during the meal,” that’s likely observable (the person knows if they texted). If you say “You were ignoring me during the meal,” they could argue that (they might say they weren’t ignoring you, just texting). The word “ignoring” implies intent; “texting” is the action. Ensure you’re simply recounting actions or words, not interpreting them. Rosenberg notes that if we fail to make a clear observation, conversations quickly devolve into disputes about reality rather than moving forward[11]. By making sure your observation is something “you and others can reasonably agree on”, you set a collaborative tone[22]. One tip: imagine showing a video of the incident to a neutral mediator – would your description match what they see in the video? If yes, you’ve got a solid observation. If not (if your statement uses words like “inappropriate” or “careless” that a video can’t show), refine further.
3.	Frame the observation as a complete, concise sentence:
The final step is to present your observation in a clear sentence (or two) that you could actually say or write to someone. Often, NVC practitioners use the format “When I see/heard/noticed ___, ...” to introduce an observation. You can use a phrase like “I noticed that…” or “When [at X time] you [did Y]…” to set the stage. For example: “When I came home from work today at 6 PM, I saw the garbage bag still sitting by the door.” This is a full observation statement you could communicate. It might feel strange to stop there without immediately saying how you feel about it – but remember, in the NVC structure, the next step would be stating your feeling (“...I felt frustrated”), after the observation. As an observation standalone, it’s perfectly okay if it sounds incomplete or overly factual. The key is it contains only the factual part. Ensure you’ve included all necessary context so it’s understandable: a time reference, the specific action, maybe the actors involved, and any relevant detail. Keep it concise; if it’s too long or has multiple clauses with “and,” check that you aren’t sneaking in multiple observations or an evaluation. Each sentence should contain one clear observation. You might say, for instance: “During our conversation last night, you answered your phone and started a 5-minute call while I was mid-sentence.” Period. That’s an observation. Delivered calmly, such a statement invites the listener to acknowledge the event, rather than defend themselves against a charge. Craft your final sentence in this spirit – neutral, specific, and lean. Once you have it, you’ve successfully completed a purely observational statement that Marshall Rosenberg would approve of!
By following these steps in order (context -> describe action -> strip judgments -> use specifics -> double-check neutrality -> finalize phrasing), you will end up with a statement that is purely observational. It can be helpful to literally fill in blanks as a practice exercise, such as:
•	“When/On [day/time] at [place], I saw/heard [person] do/say [X].”
For example: “On Tuesday afternoon in the break room, I heard you say ‘This project is stupid’ about my proposal.” Or “When I opened the fridge this morning, I saw that the milk carton was empty and put back on the shelf.” These templates force you to include concrete details (time, place, action). You can adjust wording later for smoothness, but the core should remain factual. Always remember to stop the sentence before you insert any opinion – a common mistake is to add “and that was inappropriate” or “which made me upset” in the same breath. Save those parts for later. The observation itself should end before any “judgment words” appear.
Examples: Transforming Evaluations into Observations
To further clarify the difference, here are several examples of evaluative statements vs. purely observational statements. These illustrate how to turn a judgment-laden comment into a neutral description of facts:
•	Evaluation: “You’re always procrastinating on your work.”
Observation: “For the past 3 assignments, you submitted the work one day after the deadline.”[23][24]
Why: “Always procrastinating” is a general judgment. The observation pinpoints a verifiable pattern (three late submissions) without the label “procrastinating.”
•	Evaluation: “The speaker was unprofessional.”
Observation: “The speaker arrived 50 minutes late to his presentation and was dressed in a T-shirt.”[25]
Why: “Unprofessional” is a subjective standard. The observation describes the concrete behaviors (lateness and attire) that led to that impression.
•	Evaluation: “Charlie behaved selfishly.”
Observation: “Charlie skipped to the front of the queue ahead of others waiting.”[26]
Why: “Selfishly” judges Charlie’s character. The observation narrates exactly what Charlie did, which anyone present could see.
•	Evaluation: “My father is a good man.”
Observation: “For the last 25 years, my father has given 10% of his salary to charity.”[17]
Why: “Good man” is a personal value judgment. The observation shares a factual example of his behavior that exemplifies what “good” means to you, without using the word “good.”
•	Evaluation: “Janice works too much.”
Observation: “Janice spent over 60 hours at the office this week, including Saturday.”[20]
Why: “Too much” is an opinion (too much for whom?). The observation provides a measurable fact – 60+ hours, which is clearly a lot, but it lets the listener conclude its impact.
•	Evaluation: “Henry is aggressive.”
Observation: “Yesterday, Henry hit his sister on the arm when she changed the TV channel.”[19]
Why: “Aggressive” labels Henry’s personality. The observation describes a specific aggressive action he took, without the label. It’s clear, if undesirable, behavior.
•	Evaluation: “You’re always late.”
Observation: “In the last two weeks, you arrived between 5–15 minutes late to our meetings on three occasions.”
Why: “Always late” will likely prompt “No, I’m not always!” The observation gives a timeframe and frequency that the person can recognize and is less exaggerative. It avoids “always” (which is rarely literally true) and focuses on specific instances.
•	Evaluation: “My son often doesn’t brush his teeth.”
Observation: “Twice this week, my son didn’t brush his teeth before bed.”[21]
Why: “Often doesn’t” is vague; stating “twice this week” is concrete. It turns a general complaint into a note of exactly when it happened.
•	Evaluation: “My aunt complains whenever I talk to her.”
Observation: “This week, my aunt called me three times, and each time she talked about people who treated her in ways she didn’t like.”[27]
Why: Saying someone “complains” is your take on their behavior. Detailing what she actually says (talking about people mistreating her) over specific calls paints the picture of complaining without using the word “complains.” It also drops “whenever I talk to her” (a sweeping generalization) in favor of a week’s concrete record.
•	Evaluation: “You’re destroying our child’s self-esteem!”
Observation: “When you said to our son, ‘You’re lazy and will never amount to anything,’ …”[28] (This would be followed by how I feel as a parent.)
Why: “Destroying our child’s self-esteem” is a dramatic evaluation of impact. The observation quotes the actual hurtful words the person said to the child. It focuses on the action (the remark made), not the outcome or judgment of it. This was the example Rachelle Lamb gave, where the observation is literally quoting the person’s words to keep it factual[28]. Anyone present would agree those words were indeed spoken.
Each of these examples shows how the same situation can be described in two ways: one that judges or generalizes, and one that simply states what happened. Notice how the observational versions tend to be longer or more detailed – that’s often necessary to remove the shorthand of judgments. We trade brevity like “aggressive” or “lazy” for a more specific description of actions. It might feel less “punchy,” but that’s exactly the point: an observation should not throw a punch. It should be as neutral as possible. By practicing with examples like these, you train yourself to spot the difference. (Tip: If you’re working on an observation builder tool, you might incorporate an exercise where users transform a few sample evaluations into observations to sharpen this skill.)
Tips for Using the Observation Builder (Guidance and Flagging)
When implementing this on an observation builder page or in your personal practice, here are some practical tips to guide users and flag issues in real time:
•	Flag generalizations and absolutes: If a user types words like “always,” “never,” “constantly,” “every time,” or “none,” consider highlighting those. Prompt them to replace or clarify with a specific example or a quantifiable detail[16]. For instance, “always late” could trigger a hint: “Can you recall a recent specific time they were late? How many times this week?” This nudges the user toward specificity.
•	Detect judgmental adjectives: Configure the tool to catch words that are typically judgments. Common ones: lazy, selfish, rude, disrespectful, careless, unkind, aggressive, inconsiderate, ignorant, etc. If such a word appears, the builder can suggest: “This word expresses an interpretation. What did the person do or say that leads you to use this word? Describe that instead.” For example, highlight “selfish” and advise: “Try describing the action. e.g. What exactly did they do that you consider selfish?”
•	Watch for name-calling or labels: Any direct insults or labels (jerk, idiot, liar, angel, drama queen, etc.) should be flagged as non-observations. The tool can gently remind: “Observations stick to behaviors and words, not labels. Please describe what was said or done.” Even positive labels like “hero” or “amazing” are evaluations (though positive) – the observation behind “You’re an amazing team player” might be “You helped each team member with their tasks this week without being asked.”
•	Highlight subjective phrases: Phrases like “for no reason,” “on purpose,” “on my behalf,” “to me,” or “to annoy me” often indicate we’re adding our spin. For example, “He left the room to cut me off” includes an assumed intent. The tool could flag “to cut me off” and ask the user to stick to what they literally observed (e.g. “he left the room abruptly in the middle of our discussion”). Essentially, any clause starting with “because…” or “to [verb]…” after describing an action might be suspect. Those usually signal why we think it happened, not what happened.
•	Encourage “I noticed…” or time/place starters: To help users, the interface can prompt a structure. For example, having input fields like [When/Where] and [What happened]. The user might fill “Yesterday at dinner” in one field and “you commented ‘This food is awful’ about the meal I cooked” in the next. This ensures they include a when/where and a what. If the site is interactive, providing a template such as “When [who] [action] at [time/place]…” could guide them. For instance, they could get a partially filled sentence: “When you __, at __” to emphasize including those elements. (They can also phrase it in first person, e.g. “When I saw/heard ...”)
•	Provide examples as live references: It can be helpful to show a few example observation statements (like those above or others relevant to your domain) on the page. Users can compare their attempt to the examples. For instance, showing “You arrived 15 minutes after our planned time on Monday” as a model might inspire someone to change “You were late as usual” into a similar factual format. Real examples act as a guide and also normalize the kind of language we’re asking for (since it can feel unnatural at first).
•	Explain why a flag is raised: When the tool flags something, include a brief explanation referencing NVC principles so the user learns. For example, “We avoid ‘always’ because it’s rarely literally true and can sound like an exaggeration. Observations work best with specific instances.” Or “Describing someone as ‘unfriendly’ is an interpretation. What did they do that leads you to that conclusion? Mention that action instead.” This way, the builder page isn’t just policing the input but also teaching the user Marshall Rosenberg’s methodology as they use it.
•	Reinforce separating feelings from the observation: If a user writes a sentence that includes “I feel” or an emotion word, the tool might separate that out. For example, “When you didn’t say hello this morning, I felt hurt.” While this is a valid full NVC expression (observation + feeling), the observation part alone is “When you didn’t say hello this morning”. The tool can acknowledge the feeling but perhaps prompt: “Great, you included a feeling. For the observation step, let’s just note the facts first: ‘When you didn’t say hello this morning.’ We’ll address ‘I felt hurt’ in the next step (feelings).” This teaches the user the discipline of handling one component at a time, as Rosenberg intended[1].
By guiding users in this way, your observation builder will not only flag problematic language but also coach them toward successful rephrasing. It’s like having a tutor looking over your shoulder, reminding you of Rosenberg’s lessons. Over time, users will internalize these patterns – they’ll start naturally thinking “OK, how can I say this without the judgment?”
Conclusion: Practicing Observations for Compassionate Communication
Crafting purely observational statements is a skill that gets easier with practice, but it requires a shift in mindset. Marshall Rosenberg’s approach asks us to pause and separate what we sense happened from our story about it. This can feel laborious initially (because our “story” comes so fast), but it is profoundly rewarding. By rigorously sticking to observations, we demonstrate a form of respect – we are describing reality as the other person might have seen it too, rather than accusing or abstracting. This opens the door for them to actually hear the next parts (our feelings and needs) without getting stuck on defending themselves[7][11].
Remember Rosenberg’s assurance: NVC isn’t about stifling all your judgments or pretending not to have opinions. It’s about timing and clarity – first laying out the observation, then later expressing your evaluation (indirectly through feelings about how the action affected you, or directly if needed)[4]. By keeping that separation, you honor both the reality of what happened and your own subjective experience, in turn inviting the other person to do the same.
In summary, to be “as true to his methodology as possible,” continually ask yourself: “Am I observing, or already judging?” If the latter, gently refocus on the concrete details. Use the fill-in-the-blank formulas and steps as training wheels. Over time, making observations without evaluation will become more natural – you’ll notice when your language slips into criticism, and you’ll know how to rephrase on the fly. As Rosenberg taught, this is a powerful form of intelligence and compassion in communication[3]. It creates a baseline of reality from which two people can connect. And as studies suggest, people who practice NVC (starting with observations) develop stronger empathy, self-awareness, and relationship satisfaction[13].
By following the comprehensive steps and tips above, you can reliably produce statements that are purely observational. This rigor in the observation stage sets the tone for the entire conversation to follow. It might not solve the conflict on its own (an observation itself isn’t warm or connecting yet[29]), but it lays the groundwork for productive dialogue. As you implement this on your site’s observation builder, you’ll be equipping users with one of the most fundamental tools in Nonviolent Communication. They’ll learn to see and describe situations the way Marshall Rosenberg encouraged us to: with clarity, honesty, and without blame[7][10]. That is the first step toward the empathy and understanding that NVC aims to foster.
